It’s official: the mountain gorilla is the only animal on Earth that’s terrified of both poachers and interior design. According to a newly unearthed memo from the 1960s, conservationist Dian Fossey once tried to save a species by luring them into a cabin with a playpen, a bunch of trees, and the existential dread of being 10,000 feet above the nearest grocery store. Spoiler: it didn’t end well for anyone.  

Fossey’s plan, as detailed in her memoir *The Gorilla’s Last Stand: Or How I Accidentally Created a Miniature Rainforest in My Living Room*, involved “recreating the natural habitat” of a baby gorilla by, uh, putting a bunch of trees in a room. Because obviously, if you want to study wildlife, you should just build a forest in your cabin and hope the animals show up. The porters, who had already been tasked with carrying a child’s playpen up a mountain, were initially confused. “Why are we bringing a playpen?” one asked. “Is this a baby shower?” Another replied, “I thought this was a safari.”  

The baby gorilla, whose name was apparently “Tiny Tragedy” (per Fossey’s notes), was allegedly “captured” after being “harassed by poachers and cattle grazers.” Which, in Fossey’s words, “was just a fancy way of saying the poachers were tired of the gorillas eating their cows.” The rescue operation involved a team of porters, a playpen, and a desperate attempt to make a cabin look like a jungle. The result? A room that smelled like damp moss, had a few vines hanging from the ceiling, and a baby gorilla who was “extremely unimpressed.”  

Fossey’s team later claimed the playpen “was the most effective gorilla trap ever invented,” though it’s unclear if the gorilla was actually trapped or just mildly annoyed. The baby, after 68 days of “forest interior design,” was eventually released back into the wild—though not before Fossey wrote a 20-page letter to the National Geographic Society complaining that “the gorillas are too feral for modern living.”  

In the end, the mountain gorilla’s fate remains uncertain. But at least now they have a 10,000-foot luxury cabin to call home.
