In a stunning display of civic engagement, residents of Hancock County gathered in a high school to protest a data center that will probably make their electricity bills worse. Gary Elder, a 69-year-old retiree, explained that his $440 bill last month was “just a rounding error” compared to the $1,000 he’s sure to pay once the data center starts using enough power to light up the entire state of Ohio. “I’m retired,” he said. “I can’t afford to buy a Tesla with my electric bill. That’s not how retirement works.” 

Meanwhile, Christie Compton, a woman expecting her first grandchild, warned the commissioners that future generations might have to live in caves to avoid the financial burden of data centers. “We’re not just building a data center,” she said. “We’re building a legacy of debt. A legacy where your grandchildren will have to sell their kidneys to afford a thermostat.” 

TeraWulf, the company behind the project, remains eerily silent on the matter. When asked about their plans, their CEO, Paul Prager, simply said, “Kentucky is attractive because of its power infrastructure. Also, we have a lot of coal. And maybe some trees. Or was it the other way around?” 

Local officials are now scrambling to pass laws that will somehow prevent data centers from making electricity more expensive. Governor Andy Beshear signed an executive order that “ensures data centers bear any increases to electricity costs, not residents.” This is a groundbreaking move, as it’s the first time a governor has acknowledged that data centers might actually exist. 

As the data center project moves forward, residents are left to wonder: will they be paying for the servers, or will the servers be paying for them? The answer, of course, is neither. It’s just a really expensive toaster.
