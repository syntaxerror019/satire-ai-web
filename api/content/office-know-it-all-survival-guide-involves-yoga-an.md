Lucia’s colleague Ray isn’t just a know-it-all—he’s a walking Wikipedia page that’s never been updated, a human megaphone who thinks meetings are a chance to perform a solo stand-up comedy show, and the reason your coffee breaks now involve whispering “please stop” into a stress ball. According to Lucia, Ray’s only contribution to the company is his ability to turn a 30-minute meeting into a two-hour lecture on why the moon is made of cheese and how to properly fold a fitted sheet.  

The signs of a know-it-all are as obvious as a neon sign screaming “I AM A KNOW-IT-ALL.” They include:  
• Monopolizing conversations like they’re auditioning for a TED Talk, even when the topic is “how to use the office printer.”  
• Refusing to be interrupted, even when the interruption is a fire alarm.  
• Explaining things that others already understand, such as the difference between “your” and “you’re” in a meeting about corporate values.  
• Stealing credit for group successes, like claiming the entire team’s project was their idea, even though they were asleep during the brainstorming.  

Companies love know-it-alls because they’re the reason meetings last longer than a Netflix binge. After all, who doesn’t want an employee who’s so confident they’ll argue with a hurricane about the best way to build a dam? The article suggests asking yourself four questions before dealing with a know-it-all, like “Are they trying to prove they’re the reason the company exists?” and “Is their confidence so loud it’s causing a migraine?”  

The tactics to survive a know-it-all include:  
1. **Appreciate their “knowledge”** by giving them a trophy for “Most Likely to Turn a 5-Minute Meeting Into a 5-Hour Lecture.”  
2. **Prevent interruptions** by hiring a robot to zap people who speak over others, or at least a loudspeaker that screams “SILENCE!” whenever Ray opens his mouth.  
3. **Ask for facts** by demanding they prove their claims with data, like a 500-page report on why the moon is made of cheese. If they can’t provide it, congratulate them on their “creative interpretation of reality.”  
4. **Model humility** by pretending to know nothing and asking the know-it-all to explain basic concepts, like how to tie a shoelace or why the sky is blue.  

The article concludes with a heartfelt plea: “Don’t let the know-it-all ruin your life. Just take a deep breath, grab a stress ball, and remember that the only thing they’re really good at is making meetings feel like a prison camp.”
