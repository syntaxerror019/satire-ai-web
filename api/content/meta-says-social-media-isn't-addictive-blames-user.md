In a stunning twist that will shock no one, Meta has officially denied that its platforms are addictive, claiming the entire concept of "social media addiction" is a myth perpetuated by people who have ever used a smartphone. "The State AGs may call this a landmark case, but their limited claims are unsubstantiated and their financial demands are vastly disproportionate," said a Meta spokesperson, who also confirmed they have no idea what "unsubstantiated" means but are confident it’s not a word they’ve ever used in a press release.  

Meta’s legal team has reportedly spent the last decade preparing for this moment by repeatedly telling themselves, "We didn’t do anything wrong," while simultaneously hiring a team of 500 lawyers to argue that "infinite scrolling" is just a fancy way of saying "scrolling." The company also insists that features like push notifications are "benign" and that the only people harmed by them are "the people who actually use them," a group they have now officially labeled as "victims of their own curiosity."  

The lawsuit, which seeks to hold Meta accountable for allegedly trapping teens in a "digital purgatory" of endless content, has been met with a surprising level of confusion from Meta’s legal department. "We stand by our record of creating strong protections for teens," said the spokesperson, who then immediately deleted their social media accounts and installed a parental control app on their phone.  

The trial, set to last seven weeks, will reportedly feature testimony from Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg, who is expected to argue that "Instagram is just a photo-sharing app" and that the company has "no idea why anyone would spend hours looking at other people’s lives." Meanwhile, the states involved are pushing for changes like removing "like counts," which they claim have caused "existential crises" among users who now feel "inadequate" when their posts receive fewer than 100 likes.  

California Attorney General Rob Bonta, who has been leading the charge, said in a statement that Meta "designed a dangerous product for young users, knew it to be dangerous, and then lied to children, families, and the community about how dangerous it was." Meta responded by sending a cease-and-desist letter to Bonta’s office, demanding he "stop using the word 'dangerous' in public" and "focus on more important issues like finding a new hobby."  

As the trial begins, observers are already speculating that the case will end in a dramatic courtroom showdown where Meta’s lawyers will argue that "the real problem is the internet" while the states will counter with "the real problem is Meta." Meanwhile, a security guard outside Meta’s headquarters has been quietly watching the proceedings and is now on the verge of quitting his job, claiming he "can’t take it anymore."  

In related news, a judge in New Mexico recently ordered Meta to pay $567 million for contributing to a youth mental health crisis, a decision Meta is appealing by arguing that "the real crisis is the amount of money we’re being asked to pay."
