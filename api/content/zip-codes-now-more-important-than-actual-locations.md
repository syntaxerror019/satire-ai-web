In a stunning turn of events that has left geographers, postal workers, and people who still think “ZIP” stands for “Zany Individual Preference” reeling, it has come to light that zip codes are no longer just arbitrary clusters of numbers—no, no, no. They are now the new currency, the new religion, and the new reason why your neighbor is suddenly yelling at you for “stepping on their 90210.”  

According to a shocking report by the *International Journal of Things That Shouldn’t Be Taken Seriously*, 78% of Americans now believe their zip code determines their worth more than their job, their relationships, or even their ability to pronounce “quinoa” without sounding like a confused toddler. “It’s not about where you live,” said one anonymous source, whose zip code is 10001. “It’s about where you *don’t* live. My cousin in 90210 thinks I’m a nobody just because I live in 90210’s spiritual opposite, which is 90210 minus 1. That’s not a zip code, that’s a *soul wound*.”  

The situation has escalated to the point where states are now fighting over zip codes like they’re the last slice of pizza at a party. Alabama, for instance, has officially declared war on 30000, which it claims is “too close to 30001 and therefore a threat to the Alabama identity.” Meanwhile, Hawaii is considering renaming its entire state to “96801” to “capitalize on the mystique of being a five-digit number that sounds like a secret code.”  

Even the federal government has been forced to act. The USPS has announced it will now issue “zip code passports” to citizens, allowing them to travel between regions without “offending the delicate numerical balance of the nation.” “We’re not just delivering mail anymore,” said a spokesperson. “We’re delivering *destiny*.”  

In related news, a new app called “ZipMate” has launched, pairing users based on their zip codes rather than interests, hobbies, or even basic human decency. “It’s like Tinder, but instead of swiping left on a boring person, you swipe left on a person from 90210,” said the app’s founder, who lives in 90210. “It’s a *movement*.”  

As the world braces for a future where your zip code might determine whether you’re allowed to vote, date, or even breathe the same air as someone else, one thing is clear: the next time you see a 90210 on a shirt, don’t just think “Hollywood.” Think “dominance.” Think “inevitability.” Think “the reason your life is suddenly a series of 5-digit numbers and existential dread.”
