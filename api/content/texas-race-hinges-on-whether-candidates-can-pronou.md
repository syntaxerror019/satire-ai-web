In what can only be described as the most technologically advanced congressional race since the invention of the wheel, Texas’ 10th District is now a battleground where candidates are judged not on their policies, but on their ability to correctly pronounce “terafab” without sounding like a confused toddler. Former America PAC lawyer Chris Gober, who once advised on the creation of the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE), is running against Caitlin Rourk in a race that’s less about healthcare and more about who can convincingly claim they’ve never heard of a “data center.”  

Gober’s campaign, funded by Silicon Valley’s most enthusiastic tech bros, has turned the race into a surreal spectacle of corporate lobbying and AI-friendly PACs. His law firm, Lex Politica, has represented everyone from Musk’s America PAC to a think tank aligned with Hungary’s former prime minister—because why stop at one political ideology when you can have a buffet of authoritarianism? Meanwhile, Gober’s side hustle, Maven Defense Solutions, seems to exist solely to confuse voters with its name, which is suspiciously similar to a project that once tried to teach machines to recognize bombs.  

The real issue isn’t AI regulation, it’s whether your candidate can pronounce “terafab” without sounding like a confused toddler. With SpaceX and Tesla planning to build a 100-million-square-foot semiconductor factory in the district, the race has become a referendum on whether America’s future depends on who can correctly spell “terafab” or if it’s just another excuse for tech bros to fund yet another PAC.  

Voters are being asked to choose between a candidate who once advised on creating a government efficiency department and someone who might actually know what a “data center” is. The stakes? Democracy. The prize? A seat in Congress. The only thing missing is a live-streamed debate where candidates must code a basic AI algorithm on stage. Because nothing says “midterm election” like a 10-hour coding challenge.
