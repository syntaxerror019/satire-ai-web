In a stunning display of capitalist ingenuity, Low Daily Beer—whose previous ventures included selling beer in actual bottles—has announced plans to open a “Field Office” inside a 20-foot-by-8-foot cargo container at the historic Belleville train depot. This marks the first time a brewery has ever considered using a structure that isn’t explicitly designed to hold beer, though the company insists it’s “just a temporary thing.”  

Tim Sullivan, the visionary behind this bold move, claims the cargo container’s “simplicity” and “minimal investment” made it the obvious choice for a second location. “I wasn’t sure I could handle another full-sized business,” he explained, “but a 20-foot box? That’s just a few more square feet than a mailbox. Easy.” The Field Office will be staffed by a single person, who will presumably also handle customer service, inventory management, and the occasional existential crisis.  

The Yards at Belleville Station, the larger project housing the Field Office, is being hailed as a “family-friendly food park” that will feature a children’s playground, a commercial kitchen, and a half-dozen other food and drink businesses—all housed in cargo containers. The depot, which hasn’t been used for train service since the 1940s, will now serve as the anchor of this “campus,” where people can gather to drink beer, eat food, and ponder the irony of their lives.  

Catherine Howley, project manager of The Yards, praised the cargo containers for their “eco-friendly” appeal, noting their historical ties to freight movement. “They’re like the original food trucks,” she said. “Except instead of moving from place to place, they’re just sitting here, pretending they’re part of a plan.”  

Low Daily’s lineup of beers includes the wildly popular Cowboy Cowboy, a “copper-colored double dry hopped hazy IPA” that somehow manages to be both “citrusy” and “liquid superhero.” Other offerings include Antler Oktoberfest, a “smooth bready sweetness” lager that’s “perfect for people who still think Oktoberfest is a type of sandwich,” and Corn Federal, a saison “made with a touch of corn” that’s “so French, it’s basically a stereotype.”  

Sullivan hopes the Field Office will open by late October, though he admits the exact hours “aren’t set” because “time is just a suggestion.” The project is expected to be completed by the time the depot’s 1888 brick restoration is finished—assuming the workers don’t all quit to open their own cargo-container-based breweries.  

As the community prepares to gather at The Yards, one thing is clear: the future of beer is not in bottles, not in cans, but in cargo containers—because why not?
